Research

I work on natural language processing and large language model (LLM) systems for high-stakes domains such as healthcare and education. My central conviction is that the ability to check a language model's output is as valuable as the output itself. I focus on making LLM-based systems not only capable, but grounded, evaluable, and trustworthy — bridging production engineering with research on reliability. I am pursuing this both as a PhD research direction in Computer Science and in my day-to-day work building clinical AI systems in industry.

Research Focus

Two questions anchor my research — spanning both the theoretical foundations of language modeling and the practical challenges of deploying AI where being wrong has real consequences.

Reliability & Interpretability of LLM-Based Evaluation

An LLM judge can score the same output differently across runs, and a single aggregate score rarely explains what went wrong. I study how design choices — decomposing a score into separate rubric dimensions, constraining an evaluator's output format, and reducing sampling temperature — determine whether its judgment can be trusted and understood by the people who must act on it.

Grounding Generation in Verified Knowledge

Retrieving the right information does not guarantee a model actually used it, and an answer can appear grounded even when it was reconstructed from memory. I study the mechanisms a system uses to decide when and what to retrieve, and how we verify that retrieved evidence is faithfully reflected in the output rather than merely consistent with it.

Reliable and Interpretable LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation for Clinical Documentation

In Preparation

Zephylin Dusengimana, et al. · First author

Applied research from work at AfiaSync · targeting a peer-reviewed venue

LLM judges can score the same clinical note differently across runs, and a single aggregate score rarely explains what specifically went wrong — a serious problem in a setting where a single error can affect patient safety. This work studies how design choices, including decomposing a score into separate rubric dimensions (accuracy, completeness, safety), constraining the evaluator's output to a fixed schema, and lowering sampling temperature, affect whether an automated judge's decisions are reliable and interpretable enough for clinicians to act on. The evaluator explicitly lists hallucinated findings and omitted real findings, and is validated against structured clinical notes generated from real encounters.
